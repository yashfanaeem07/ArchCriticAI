# Massing, Proportion, and Composition

## Massing: the building as a play of volumes

Massing is the composition of a building's primary volumes — their size, number,
and relationship — read before any detail registers. It is the first thing
perceived from the street and the last thing forgotten. The fundamental decisions
are few but consequential: one volume or several; volumes that are stacked,
linked, or split; a single gesture or an assembly of parts. A scheme whose massing
is resolved reads as inevitable from a distance; one whose massing is unresolved
appears either inert (a single undifferentiated block) or restless (an additive
pile of pieces that never settles into a whole).

## Proportion and the disciplined elevation

Proportion is the ratio of part to part and part to whole, and it is what makes a
composition feel resolved rather than arbitrary. Architects have long reached for
proportional systems — the golden section, the root rectangles, Le Corbusier's
Modulor, the Classical orders — not as mystical formulas but as disciplines that
keep the relationships between openings, bays, and volumes coherent. A well-
proportioned elevation rewards sustained looking: its windows, solids, and voids
sit in clear ratio, and the eye senses an underlying order even without measuring
it. Proportion is also how scale is communicated — the same facade can read as
intimate or monumental depending on the size and rhythm of its parts.

## Symmetry, balance, and duality

Symmetry is the most direct way to declare order and importance; a symmetrical
composition centres attention and reads as formal, civic, or stable. But symmetry
is not the only path to balance. *Asymmetrical balance* distributes visual weight
without mirroring — a tall slender element answered by a long low one — and often
feels more dynamic and contemporary. *Duality*, the deliberate pairing or
opposition of two elements (solid against void, heavy against light, open against
closed), can structure a whole project: two volumes split by a glazed seam, a
served and a servant zone, a public and a private register. The critic reads the
degree of symmetry in a composition as a cue to its formal intentions.

## Figure, ground, and the composition of voids

Composition is not only the arrangement of solids but the shaping of the voids
between and within them. The courtyard, the slot, the carved recess, and the
framed sky are designed figures as much as the walls that bound them. Mature
composition treats poche — the thickness of wall and the residual space — as
active material, so that the void reads as intentional rather than left over. A
strong figure–ground relationship lets the eye distinguish the primary volume from
its setting and the carved space from the surrounding mass.

## Common failures of composition

Composition fails when massing is additive without hierarchy, so no volume reads
as primary; when proportion is unconsidered, leaving openings and bays in
arbitrary relation; and when symmetry is applied as a reflex rather than chosen for
a reason. The remedy is to decide which volume leads, to test the elevation against
a proportional discipline, and to choose symmetry, asymmetry, or duality as a
deliberate expression of how the building is organised.
