# Materiality, Detailing, and Tectonics

## Tectonics: the art of making visible how a building stands

Tectonics is the expression of construction — the way a building shows how it is
made and how it stands up. Kenneth Frampton argued that architecture's deepest
meaning lies not in its image but in its joints: the connection of beam to column,
of wall to ground, of roof to sky. A tectonic project makes its structural and
material logic legible; you can read where loads gather, how members meet, and why
the building holds together. The opposite is the *scenographic* building, whose
surfaces are applied like stage scenery and conceal rather than reveal how it is
built.

## Material as a carrier of meaning

Every material arrives with associations and behaviours: timber is warm, tactile,
and ages by weathering; concrete is massive, plastic, and records its formwork;
steel is precise and slender; glass is immaterial and reflective. Choosing a
material is choosing a set of meanings and a way of building. The strongest
schemes use few materials, deployed consistently, so that each does identifiable
work — a structural palette and a cladding palette, rather than a catalogue of
finishes. A project that names "timber structure" in its concept but renders as a
flat, monochrome volume has undersold its own material thesis; the tectonics must
be shown, not merely asserted.

## The joint as the locus of detail

"God is in the details," Mies van der Rohe insisted, and the detail he meant was
the joint. Detailing is where the general idea is tested against the reality of
two materials meeting: the reveal that separates a window from its wall, the
shadow gap that lets a plane read as independent, the threshold strip where floor
meets door. Good detailing is not ornament added at the end; it is the resolution
of the concept at the smallest scale. A deep reveal can carry a threshold parti
down to the doorway; a continuous datum line can be held by an aligned course of
brick. When details contradict the parti — when joints are arbitrary or
expedient — the project loses coherence at the scale a visitor actually touches.

## Texture, weathering, and the life of surfaces

Materials are not static; they age, weather, and patinate. Concrete streaks,
copper greens, timber silvers, white render stains. Mature design anticipates this
life rather than ignoring it — detailing drips and overhangs so water leaves
intended marks, choosing materials that improve rather than degrade with time.
Texture also governs how light behaves: a rough board-marked wall catches raking
light and reveals its grain, while a polished surface dissolves into reflection.
Material variety and tonal range register, in a drawing, as the richness of the
proposed surfaces.

## Common failures of materiality

Materiality fails when it is decorative rather than tectonic — finishes chosen for
image with no relationship to how the building is made. It fails when the palette
is undifferentiated, leaving structure, enclosure, and detail visually
indistinguishable. And it fails when the detail is unresolved, so the careful
idea at the scale of the plan collapses at the scale of the joint. The remedy is
to let construction be visible, to limit the palette, and to design the joints as
deliberately as the rooms.
