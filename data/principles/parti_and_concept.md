# Parti and Concept-Driven Design

## The parti as organising idea

The *parti* (from the French *prendre parti*, "to make a decision") is the
central organising idea of a building — the single diagram from which the rest of
the design follows. A strong parti is reductive: it can be drawn in a few lines
and stated in one sentence. "A public route threaded through the building toward
the water" or "two solid volumes split by a glazed seam" are partis. They are not
the building, but the rule the building obeys.

A concept-driven project is legible because every major move can be traced back to
the parti. When a jury says a scheme "reads clearly," they mean the parti survives
translation from sketch to plan to section to model. When they say it is "busy" or
"additive," they usually mean the parti has been lost beneath decisions that don't
serve it. The test of a concept is not how clever it sounds but how much of the
project it explains.

## Concept versus theme

A concept is generative; a theme is decorative. A concept changes where walls go,
how light enters, and how people move. A theme — "nautical," "forest," "origami" —
is applied to a building whose organisation would be the same without it. Studio
critics push students from theme toward concept by asking, repeatedly, "what does
this idea make you do?" If the answer is "nothing structural," it is a theme.

## Carrying the parti through scales

The strongest projects repeat their organising idea at several scales — in the
site strategy, the plan, the section, and the detail. A threshold concept might
appear as a colonnade at the scale of the city, a compression-and-release sequence
at the scale of the room, and a deep reveal at the scale of the doorway. This
self-similarity is what gives a building coherence; it is the architectural
equivalent of a well-developed musical motif.

## Common failures of concept

Concepts most often fail in three ways. First, the *abandoned* parti: a clear idea
at ground level that dissolves on the upper floors, which become merely stacked
program. Second, the *competing* parti: two strong ideas that never resolve into
one, leaving the project legible as neither. Third, the *unsupported* parti: a
diagram with no programmatic or structural reason to exist, which the jury reads
as arbitrary. The remedy in each case is the same — decide which idea is primary
and subordinate or remove the rest.
